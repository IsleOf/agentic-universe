## The trap it had to avoid
Model *mechanism* as an unevadable constraint and *preference* as an internal weight an agent
may not share, and mechanism wins **by definition** — the rig would prove nothing. So each
lever carries its real cost:

| lever | strength | cost |
|---|---|---|
| **mechanism** | binds non-adopters — the constraint is external | **coarse** (one cap for heterogeneous agents) and needs **monitoring** |
| **preference** | fine-grained, no deadweight loss, no monitoring | **completely inert** on an agent that never adopted it |

## Results (sustain rate / mean yield, 200 episodes per cell)
```
def=0.0     none 0.00/70   mechanism 1.00/223   preference 1.00/285   both 1.00/220
def=0.125   none 0.00/70   mechanism 1.00/223   preference 0.72/252   both 1.00/221
def=0.25    none 0.00/70   mechanism 1.00/223   preference 0.04/132   both 1.00/222
def=0.50    none 0.00/70   mechanism 1.00/224   preference 0.01/87    both 1.00/222
```

- **Full adoption:** preference out-yields the cap **285 vs 223** (~28% more) — fine-grained
  restraint has no deadweight loss. Both sustain.
- **Crossover at 12.5% defection** — *one agent in eight*. Preference sustain falls 1.00 → 0.72;
  by 25% it has collapsed to 0.04 while mechanism still holds at 1.00.
- **Monitoring is what mechanism pays.** At `detect=0.9` it loses **0.65 of sustain rate** — a
  cap with little headroom cannot absorb even occasional evasion.

## The result I did not predict
**Stacking both levers gives mechanism's yield, not preference's.** `both` sustains everywhere
but yields ~220 — no better than mechanism alone, and well below preference's 285. The cap
binds first, so the preference model adds nothing on top.

Practically: **if you expect any defection at all, you are choosing mechanism's yield whether
or not you also do preference engineering.** The levers do not compose additively. That is
asserted by a selftest check, so it can't quietly stop being true.
