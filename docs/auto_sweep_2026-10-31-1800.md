## The finding is counterintuitive
Across three environments where agents sabotage while evading monitors:

> **Contemporary monitors often perform BETTER with LESS information** — the *less-is-more
> effect for automated oversight.*

The natural expectation — a monitor with more of the agent's reasoning and actions does better
— is **wrong** for current systems.

Their method: **extract-and-evaluate (EaE)** — one monitor isolates the relevant excerpts, a
second scores only those. **+16.8 percentage points** over the next-best approach on
BigCodeBench-Sabotage (GPT-4.1-mini monitor). Two more results: agents **unaware** of being
monitored are caught much more easily, and monitors on **longer** traces gain **more** from
filtering.

## The risk the abstract doesn't address
Recorded as an open question rather than asserted, and written into `cohort_monitor.py` so it
travels with the advice:

**The extractor becomes a single point of failure.** If it misses the out-of-scope behaviour,
the scorer never sees it — and the pipeline then reports clean with **more** confidence than an
unfiltered monitor would have. Filtering converts a noisy-signal problem into a **recall**
problem at the extraction step. Whether the 16.8-point gain survives an adversary optimising
against the **extractor** rather than the scorer is not something this abstract answers.

Worth noting the cohort monitor is *already* EaE-shaped one level up: each worker yields one
scalar and the pool score is computed over those, not over the union of transcripts.
